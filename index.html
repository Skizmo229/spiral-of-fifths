The Circle of Fifths Is a Spiral

Stack pure fifths from C and you never get back to C. Twelve steps lands you on B♯, which is a slightly higher note. Keep going and the spiral winds outward through more and more sharps. Go the other way and it winds inward through more and more flats. Neither end ever stops.

Why it's a spiral, not a circle

1 · The names never repeat

Start on C and climb in fifths: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯… and the twelfth step is B♯, not C. Keep going: F𝄪, C𝄪, G𝄪… then triple sharps, quadruple sharps, forever. Going down: F, B♭, E♭… C♭, F♭, B𝄫… forever. B♯ and C share a piano key, but they are not the same note.

2 · The sound doesn't come back either

A pure fifth is a 3 : 2 frequency ratio. Stack twelve of them: (3/2)12 ≈ 129.746. Seven octaves: 27 = 128. So B♯ comes out about 1.4% higher than C. That's 23.46 cents, a gap called the Pythagorean comma. Play the two together and you can hear it as a slow wobble.

3 · And it never will

To land exactly back home you'd need 3a = 2b for some whole numbers a and b. But every power of 3 is odd and every power of 2 is even, so they can never be equal. There are near misses (53 fifths overshoot 31 octaves by only 3.6 cents), but a miss is a miss. The spiral goes on forever outward, adding sharps, and forever inward, adding flats.

4 · How the piano fakes a circle

Equal temperament makes every fifth 700 cents instead of 701.955, shaving 1.955 cents off each one. Twelve shaved fifths make exactly seven octaves. The comma gets spread too thinly to notice, and the spiral is squashed flat into the familiar 12-note circle, where B♯, C and D𝄫 all become one key. Press Equal temperament to watch it collapse. Press ET rings to keep each lap on a ring of its own instead: the rings line up on 12 spokes, one for each piano key, so B♯, C and D𝄫 share a spoke. Or try other tunings in the Tuning tab.

5 · Where the 3 : 2 comes from

Every note an instrument plays is really a stack of harmonics: whole-number multiples of its lowest frequency. Here are the first eight for a low C. Click one to hear it on its own.

The 2nd harmonic is the octave. The 3rd, brought down an octave, is the fifth: the simplest new note there is, which is why the whole spiral is built from it. The 5th harmonic gives the natural major third (5 : 4), which stacking fifths never quite reaches: four pure fifths make 81 : 64, about 22 cents too wide. The 7th is a "blue" seventh that no piano key has. Played together, all eight melt into a single C.

Reading the picture

  • Going around is distance travelled in fifths. With pure fifths one step is 30.08°, a hair more than a twelfth of a turn, so a lap of 12 comes to 361°. Each note sits about 1° past its partner one lap in. That sliver is the comma.
  • Out and in are sharps and flats. It's a logarithmic spiral: every lap is the same shape at a different size, so there are infinitely many laps in both directions and it never reaches the centre.
  • Colour: ivory for naturals, warm for sharps, cool for flats. Deeper colour means more accidentals. ♯5 means five sharps.
  • A green ring marks the selected note, and dashed green rings mark the notes a whole lap away from it. With 12 notes per lap they share its piano key: same key, different notes.
  • The faint spokes are where the notes would sit if the circle closed. With 12 per lap they're the piano's keys as an ordinary circle of fifths. In ET rings every note sits exactly on one, a ring for each lap.

Exploring with the tabs

  • Key: a scale is a run of neighbouring notes on the spiral: 5 for pentatonic, 7 for major, minor and the modes, 12 for chromatic. Slide it along and the key signature changes one sharp or flat at a time.
  • Interval: every interval is a fixed number of fifths. A major third is always 4 steps out and a minor third 3 steps in, whatever note you start on.
  • Chord: every chord type is a fixed shape, and changing key just slides it along the spiral. The chord buttons build on whichever note is selected, and Flats and Sharps play the chord on every note they pass.
  • Tuning: change the size of the fifth and watch the spiral open, close, or drift the other way.

Controls

  • Scroll wheel or ← →: move one fifth. Shift + ← →: one whole lap. ↑ ↓: jump 12 fifths. With Auto-play on, jumps sound only the note you land on. The arrow keys carry on from the note you clicked last.
  • Instrument and Volume, in the bottom bar: the sound notes are played with, and how loud. Every instrument is in exact tune, with no vibrato, so small tuning differences stay easy to hear. Barber pole and the harmonics above always use sine waves.
  • Enter or Space: play the note you've moved to, the same as clicking it.
  • Drag outward or inward to travel. Pinch, the Zoom slider or + / − to zoom; 0 resets the zoom.
  • H home · A auto-play · B barber pole · T pure ↔ equal temperament · R equal temperament as one circle ↔ rings · 1–5 switch tabs · I close or bring back the title panel · Esc close this panel.
  • Notes that pop up go away by themselves. To clear one sooner, tap it or press Esc.
  • Your tuning, tab, zoom, key and chord are remembered for next time.

The barber-pole sound

Normally every note is played near middle C, so climbing in fifths has to jump back down an octave every so often. With Barber pole on, each note is a Shepard tone: the same note in every octave at once, loudest in the middle and fading to nothing at the top and bottom. Each step glides every octave up (or down) a fifth. The highest ones fade out as new ones fade in underneath, so the pitch seems to climb or sink forever while staying in the same place. The steps glide rather than jump, because a jump of a fifth between Shepard tones can easily sound like a step down a fourth.

Sound: notes are played near middle C (C4 = 261.63 Hz).
